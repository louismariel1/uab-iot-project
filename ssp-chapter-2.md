 # 2\. Target Users and Stakeholders

 ## 2.1 Purpose of the Chapter

 The effectiveness of an IoT perimeter-monitoring solution depends not only on its technical architecture but also on how the system fits the needs and responsibilities of the people and organizations that use, operate, manage, procure or regulate it.

 SSP is therefore designed as a multi-stakeholder platform. Its different users do not have the same objectives, access rights or information requirements. A protected person may primarily require timely and reliable protection, while an authorized operator requires situational awareness and actionable alerts. An organization deploying SSP requires reliable operation, manageable costs and scalability, while technical and regulatory stakeholders require security, privacy, traceability and compliance.

 This chapter identifies the principal SSP stakeholders and defines their respective roles, needs and interactions with the system. These characteristics will subsequently be translated into functional, technical, security, privacy and performance requirements in Chapter 3.

---

 ## 2.2 Primary Users

 The primary users are the individuals or operational entities that directly depend on SSP for perimeter monitoring and protection.

 ### 2.2.1 Protected Person

 A protected person is an individual for whom SSP is deployed to provide protection against a defined perimeter or proximity threat.

 Depending on the application, the protected person may be associated with a predefined protection zone. The system may monitor whether a monitored device enters, approaches or otherwise violates the configured protection conditions.

 The protected person may interact with SSP through a dedicated device, mobile application or other authorized interface. However, the system should not require the protected person to perform complex technical operations during normal operation.

 Key needs include:

 - reliable protection-zone monitoring;
- timely notification of relevant events;
- simple and understandable interaction;
- high availability;
- protection against unauthorized access to personal information;
- reliable operation during temporary communication problems;
- appropriate escalation when a critical event is detected.

 The protected person is therefore primarily concerned with **protection effectiveness, reliability, privacy and timely notification**.

 ### 2.2.2 Monitored Person

 The monitored person is an individual carrying or associated with the SSP monitoring device in an authorized monitoring scenario.

 The system may monitor parameters such as:

 - position;
- movement;
- proximity to defined zones;
- device status;
- connectivity;
- battery condition;
- tamper-related events.

 The monitored person's interaction with the system should be limited to appropriate operational functions and should not provide unauthorized access to protected information or system configuration.

 Because SSP may process sensitive location and movement information, monitoring must be governed by the applicable legal, operational and privacy framework.

 The monitored person is therefore an important stakeholder even though they are not necessarily an operator of the system.

---

 ## 2.3 Operational Users

 ### 2.3.1 Monitoring Operator

 The monitoring operator is responsible for observing active cases and responding to alerts generated by SSP.

 The operator may access a centralized dashboard showing information such as:

 - active devices;
- device status;
- location or location confidence where authorized;
- current risk state;
- battery status;
- connectivity status;
- active alerts;
- historical events;
- system-health information.

 The operator should not be required to continuously interpret raw sensor data. SSP should transform low-level device and edge information into operationally meaningful events and alerts.

 The primary operational requirement is therefore:

 > **The system should provide the right information to the authorized operator at the appropriate time and with sufficient context to support an operational response.**

 ### 2.3.2 Supervisor / Case Manager

 A supervisor or case manager may require a broader view than an individual monitoring operator.

 Typical functions may include:

 - reviewing active cases;
- reviewing historical events;
- managing monitoring policies;
- reviewing incident records;
- supervising operator activity;
- configuring authorized thresholds;
- reviewing system performance;
- generating reports.

 This role requires greater access to historical and analytical information but should remain subject to role-based authorization.

 ### 2.3.3 System Administrator

 The system administrator is responsible for the technical and operational configuration of the SSP platform.

 Responsibilities may include:

 - device registration;
- device provisioning;
- user-account management;
- communication configuration;
- software and firmware management;
- security configuration;
- system monitoring;
- backup and recovery;
- infrastructure management.

 Administrative access should be separated from operational access so that technical privileges do not automatically provide unrestricted access to sensitive case information.

---

 ## 2.4 Customers and Deploying Organizations

 SSP may be deployed by organizations responsible for protecting people, facilities or defined geographical areas.

 Potential customers include:

 - public-sector organizations;
- judicial or authorized monitoring services;
- security organizations;
- educational institutions;
- organizations operating sensitive facilities;
- organizations responsible for protected-person services;
- other authorized entities requiring controlled perimeter monitoring.

 The customer or deploying organization is primarily concerned with the overall value and sustainability of the system.

 Its requirements may include:

 - clearly defined protection capabilities;
- reliability and availability;
- security and privacy;
- predictable operating costs;
- manageable deployment;
- scalability;
- integration with existing systems;
- maintainability;
- regulatory compliance;
- measurable performance.

 The customer therefore evaluates SSP not only as an IoT device but as an **end-to-end operational platform**.

 ## 2.5 Technical Stakeholders

 SSP requires several technical stakeholders to design, deploy, maintain and evolve the complete system.

 ### 2.5.1 IoT / Embedded Engineers

 Responsible for:

 - device architecture;
- MCU/SoC selection;
- sensor integration;
- firmware;
- power management;
- device communications;
- local intelligence;
- hardware security.

 ### 2.5.2 Edge Engineers

 Responsible for:

 - edge computing infrastructure;
- local data processing;
- predictive algorithms;
- local decision-making;
- communication optimization;
- offline operation;
- edge security.

 ### 2.5.3 Cloud / Backend Engineers

 Responsible for:

 - cloud infrastructure;
- APIs;
- databases;
- event processing;
- authentication;
- fleet management;
- cloud analytics;
- system scalability.

 ### 2.5.4 AI / Data Engineers

 Responsible for:

 - data preparation;
- model development;
- model evaluation;
- model deployment;
- model monitoring;
- model updating;
- fleet-level analytics.

 AI functionality should remain subject to defined operational requirements. Models should therefore be evaluated not only for predictive performance but also for computational requirements, latency, energy consumption, robustness and privacy implications.

 ### 2.5.5 Cybersecurity Engineers

 Responsible for the security architecture across all three SSP layers.

 Their responsibilities include:

 - device authentication;
- secure communication;
- key management;
- secure firmware updates;
- access control;
- intrusion and anomaly detection;
- logging;
- vulnerability management;
- incident response.

 ### 2.5.6 System Integrators

 System integrators connect SSP to the wider operational environment.

 Potential integration points include:

 - existing monitoring platforms;
- notification systems;
- institutional information systems;
- identity-management systems;
- communication infrastructure;
- external APIs.

 Interoperability is therefore an important architectural consideration.

 ## 2.6 Regulatory and Institutional Stakeholders

 Because SSP can process sensitive location, movement and incident information, deployment may involve regulatory and institutional stakeholders.

 Depending on the application and jurisdiction, these may include:

 - public authorities;
- judicial institutions;
- data-protection authorities;
- law-enforcement or authorized monitoring organizations;
- institutional security departments;
- legal and compliance teams.

 These stakeholders may establish requirements concerning:

 - lawful deployment;
- data protection;
- data minimization;
- retention periods;
- access control;
- auditability;
- incident handling;
- system certification;
- interoperability;
- operational accountability.

 The SSP architecture should therefore support the principle that access to information is determined by **authorized role and operational need**, rather than by technical availability alone.

 ## 2.8 SSP User Personas

 To translate stakeholder requirements into practical system behavior, four representative personas can be used during the design process.

 ### Persona 1 — Protected Person

 **Role:** Person requiring protection within a defined geographical area.

 **Primary objective:** Receive reliable protection without having to manage the technical system.

 **Needs:**

 - timely alerts;
- simple interaction;
- reliable communication;
- privacy;
- confidence that the system remains operational.

 **Key SSP design implications:**

 - simple user interaction;
- high availability;
- clear alerts;
- resilient communication;
- privacy-aware processing.

---

 ### Persona 2 — Monitoring Operator

 **Role:** Authorized professional responsible for monitoring active cases.

 **Primary objective:** Detect and respond to relevant events efficiently.

 **Needs:**

 - real-time system status;
- prioritized alerts;
- location information when authorized;
- risk/context information;
- device health;
- historical event context.

 **Key SSP design implications:**

 - event prioritization;
- low alert latency;
- clear visualization;
- risk classification;
- suppression of unnecessary repetitive alerts.

---

 ### Persona 3 — System Administrator

 **Role:** Technical administrator responsible for the SSP infrastructure.

 **Primary objective:** Maintain the system securely and reliably.

 **Needs:**

 - device provisioning;
- configuration management;
- firmware management;
- system-health monitoring;
- security monitoring;
- backup and recovery.

 **Key SSP design implications:**

 - secure device identity;
- remote management;
- role-based access;
- audit logs;
- fault detection;
- secure update mechanisms.

---

 ### Persona 4 — Deploying Organization

 **Role:** Organization responsible for deploying and operating SSP.

 **Primary objective:** Obtain a reliable, secure and scalable monitoring platform.

 **Needs:**

 - predictable cost;
- measurable performance;
- scalability;
- integration;
- maintainability;
- security and privacy;
- operational reporting.

 **Key SSP design implications:**

 - modular architecture;
- standardized interfaces;
- scalable cloud architecture;
- measurable KPIs;
- lifecycle management;
- defined TCO.

 ## 2.9 Stakeholder Interaction Model

 The principal interaction between the SSP stakeholders and the system can be represented as:

 **Protected / monitored person → SSP Device → Edge → Cloud → Authorized operator**

 with the corresponding control and configuration path:

 **Authorized organization / administrator → Cloud policies → Edge configuration → Device configuration**

 A more complete representation is:

 **Person / Environment → Device sensing → Edge intelligence → Cloud intelligence → Authorized user → Operational action**

 while feedback follows:

 **Operational policy → Cloud → Edge → Device → Adaptive monitoring**

 This interaction model establishes an important principle for the subsequent architecture:

 > SSP is not simply a device that sends information to a server. It is a coordinated system in which sensing, intelligence, communication, policy and operational response form a continuous end-to-end loop.

 ## 2.10 Access and Responsibility Model

 Because SSP handles potentially sensitive operational information, access should be separated according to responsibility.

 A conceptual access model is:

 | Function | Protected person | Operator | Supervisor | Administrator | Technical team |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Receive relevant alerts | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | — |
| View assigned operational status | Limited | ✓ | ✓ | Limited | — |
| Review historical events | Limited | ✓ | ✓ | — | Authorized |
| Configure monitoring policies | — | Limited | ✓ | ✓ | Authorized |
| Manage devices | — | Limited | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Manage infrastructure | — | — | — | ✓ | ✓ |
| Manage AI models | — | — | — | Limited | ✓ |
| Security administration | — | — | — | ✓ | ✓ |
| System-wide analytics | — | Limited | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

The precise permissions will depend on the deployment environment and applicable legal and organizational requirements. The table therefore represents the **architectural principle** rather than a final authorization policy.

 ## 2.11 Adoption Environment

 SSP is intended to operate in environments where continuous or event-driven monitoring is required and where the consequences of communication failure, excessive false alerts, poor battery management or unauthorized information disclosure can be significant.

 The deployment environment can vary considerably. A secure facility may require fixed perimeter monitoring, while a person-protection application may require a wearable device and dynamic geographical zones. The architecture must therefore support different configurations without changing the fundamental Device–Edge–Cloud model.

 This leads to an important SSP design principle:

 > **The platform should be configurable at the policy and application level while remaining stable at the architectural level.**

 Configuration may include:

 - monitored zones;
- inclusion/exclusion rules;
- risk thresholds;
- sensing policies;
- communication priorities;
- alert rules;
- privacy policies;
- retention policies;
- user roles;
- escalation procedures.

 This configurability is essential for supporting multiple application domains while avoiding the need to create a completely different IoT platform for each deployment.

 ## 2.12 Chapter 2 Conclusion

 The stakeholder analysis establishes SSP as a multi-user IoT platform rather than a standalone monitoring device. Its users range from protected and monitored persons to operational operators, supervisors, system administrators, deploying organizations and technical teams. Regulatory and institutional stakeholders also influence how the system can be designed and deployed.

 The analysis identifies several common requirements across these stakeholders: **reliability, timely detection and notification, security, privacy, usability, interoperability, scalability and manageable cost**.

 These stakeholder needs provide the foundation for the next stage of the design. Chapter 3 will translate them into **formal functional, non-functional, security, privacy, performance, energy, reliability and scalability requirements**, with measurable acceptance criteria wherever possible.
